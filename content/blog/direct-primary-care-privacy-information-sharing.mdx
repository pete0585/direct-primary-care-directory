Choosing a Direct Primary Care (DPC) provider is partly a clinical decision and partly a trust decision. Because DPC relationships often involve a direct monthly arrangement between you and the practice, questions about how your information is handled can feel different from what you may have experienced in other care settings. This guide walks through the privacy and information-sharing questions worth asking, offers a checklist you can bring to a conversation, and uses clearly labeled hypothetical examples to show how those questions might play out.

This article is general information for people researching Direct Primary Care services. It is not legal advice, not medical advice, and not a substitute for consulting qualified professionals about your own situation. Privacy rules can vary by location and by the specifics of a practice arrangement, so confirm details with the practice and, where appropriate, with a qualified professional such as a healthcare attorney or your state's relevant licensing or privacy authority.

## Why privacy deserves its own conversation in DPC

In many care settings, your records move through a familiar set of channels: a health system, an insurer, a referral network. In Direct Primary Care, the practice may be smaller, may not bill insurance for routine services, and may rely on a different mix of tools and vendors. That does not automatically make information handling better or worse. It simply means the pathways are different, and different pathways deserve different questions.

The goal is not to interrogate a practice. The goal is to understand, in plain language, who sees your information, where it lives, and what choices you have. A practice that welcomes these questions is often a practice that has thought them through.

## The core questions to ask

### 1. What information do you collect, and why?

Ask the practice to describe the categories of information it keeps: contact details, scheduling notes, clinical notes, messages, payment records, and any information collected through a patient portal or app. Then ask what each category is used for. A clear answer distinguishes between information needed to deliver care, information needed to run the practice, and information used for anything else.

### 2. Who can see my information inside the practice?

In a small practice, a single person may handle several roles. Ask who on the team can access your record, and whether access is limited by role. This is a reasonable question and a useful signal about how deliberately the practice manages access.

### 3. Which outside services or vendors are involved?

Many practices use third-party tools for scheduling, messaging, billing, or record storage. Ask whether any outside vendors handle your information, what categories of information they handle, and what the practice expects of them. You do not need vendor names to get a useful answer, though some practices will share them.

### 4. How is information shared with other clinicians?

Care often involves more than one clinician. Ask how the practice shares information when you are referred elsewhere, when you see a specialist, or when you want your records sent to another provider. Ask whether sharing happens only with your request or consent, and how you would make that request.

### 5. What happens if I want to move or stop care?

Ask how you would request a copy of your records, how long that typically takes, and whether there is a process you should follow. This is one of the most practical privacy questions, because it concerns your ability to take your information with you.

### 6. How do you communicate with me?

Ask which channels the practice uses for routine messages, for clinical questions, and for urgent matters. Then ask what the practice advises if you need to reach someone quickly. Understanding the intended channel helps you avoid putting sensitive details somewhere they were not meant to go.

### 7. How would I raise a concern?

Ask who handles privacy questions or complaints and how you would reach that person. A practice that can answer this quickly is usually a practice with a clear internal process.

## A checklist you can bring to the conversation

Use this as a starting point. You can copy it into your notes and check items off as you learn more.

- [ ] I understand the main categories of information the practice keeps.
- [ ] I know who inside the practice can see my record.
- [ ] I know whether outside vendors or tools are involved in handling my information.
- [ ] I understand how information is shared with other clinicians, and what role my consent plays.
- [ ] I know how to request a copy of my records and roughly how long it takes.
- [ ] I know which communication channels the practice uses for routine, clinical, and urgent matters.
- [ ] I know who to contact with a privacy question or concern.
- [ ] I have reviewed any written materials the practice provides about privacy and information handling.
- [ ] I have asked whether anything about my situation needs special handling, and I understand the practice's answer.
- [ ] I know what to do if my contact details or preferences change.

If several items remain unclear after a conversation, that is useful information. It may mean the practice has not documented its approach, or it may mean the topic simply has not come up before. Either way, you can decide whether to ask again or to keep looking.

## Hypothetical examples, clearly labeled

These examples are invented for illustration only. They are not drawn from any real practice, provider, or patient, and they do not describe actual policies, prices, or outcomes.

**Example 1: The practice that routes messages through one channel.**
Suppose a practice asks patients to send all non-urgent questions through a patient portal and to call a specific number for anything time-sensitive. A patient who understands this can avoid sending clinical details by text or personal email, where they might sit outside the practice's intended workflow. The useful takeaway is not that one channel is better than another, but that knowing the intended channel helps you use it.

**Example 2: The practice that uses an outside scheduling tool.**
Suppose a practice uses a third-party tool for booking visits. The tool may collect your name, contact details, and appointment preferences. A patient who asks about this learns which information goes to the tool and which stays with the practice. That distinction can matter if you prefer to keep certain details out of a scheduling system.

**Example 3: The patient who wants records sent to a specialist.**
Suppose you ask your DPC practice to send records to a specialist. A clear process might involve a written request, a defined turnaround, and confirmation that the records were sent. A patient who knows the process in advance can plan around it rather than discovering it in the middle of a referral.

**Example 4: The patient who wants to change practices.**
Suppose you decide to move to a different practice. Knowing in advance how to request your records, and roughly how long it takes, can make the transition smoother. This is a privacy question in the practical sense: it is about your ability to access and carry your own information.

**Example 5: The practice that limits internal access.**
Suppose a practice restricts who can view clinical notes versus scheduling details. A patient who asks about this learns that access is role-based. Whether that matters to you is a personal judgment, but the answer itself is useful context when comparing practices.

## How to weigh what you learn

There is no single right answer to these questions, and no universal standard that applies to every practice in every location. What matters is whether the answers fit your comfort level and your circumstances. Some people want a high level of detail about vendors and systems. Others mainly want to know how to reach a person and how to get their records. Both are reasonable.

As you compare practices, consider keeping a simple note for each one: what you asked, what you learned, and what remained unclear. Over a few conversations, patterns often emerge. A practice that answers clearly and consistently may feel different from one that deflects or guesses.

It also helps to revisit these questions over time. Practices change tools, teams, and processes. A question you asked a year ago may have a different answer today, and that is normal.
