# Choosing a Direct Primary Care Provider: A Practical Guide

Direct Primary Care (DPC) is a model of primary care in which the relationship between a patient and a primary care practice is structured around a membership arrangement rather than visit-by-visit billing. If you are researching DPC because you want a different kind of primary care relationship, the most useful thing you can do is slow down and compare providers carefully. This guide is a practical decision framework — not medical advice, and not a recommendation of any named provider.

Below, we walk through the dimensions that tend to matter most when people evaluate DPC options: credentials, relevant experience, appointment format, accessibility, and transparent pricing. We finish with a checklist you can bring to your own research.

## Start with what you actually need

Before comparing anyone, get clear on your own situation. DPC practices are not interchangeable, and the right fit depends on what you want from primary care. Consider:

- **The kind of relationship you want.** Do you want longer visits, more continuity with one clinician, and easier day-to-day access? Or do you mainly need occasional routine care?
- **Your health context.** If you manage ongoing conditions, the depth of a clinician's relevant experience may matter more to you than convenience features.
- **Your practical constraints.** Location, hours, and how you prefer to communicate (in person, phone, message) all shape whether a practice will actually work for you.
- **Your budget and how you think about it.** DPC arrangements typically involve a recurring membership fee. Understanding how that fits with any insurance you hold is part of the decision.

Writing these down first will make the rest of your comparison much faster.

## 1. Credentials

Credentials are the baseline. They tell you whether a clinician is qualified to practice, and in what capacity.

Questions worth asking:

- What is the clinician's license and training background?
- Are they board-certified in a primary care specialty, and if so, which one?
- Who else is on the care team, and what are their roles?
- How does the practice handle referrals when you need care outside primary care?

You can typically verify licensing and board status through official sources rather than relying on marketing language. If a practice's website is vague about who will actually be seeing you, that is worth clarifying directly.

## 2. Relevant experience

Experience is not just years in practice — it is fit between a clinician's background and your needs.

Consider:

- **Scope of experience.** Does the clinician routinely work with people whose situation resembles yours?
- **Continuity.** How long do patients typically stay with the practice? Continuity is often cited as a core appeal of DPC, so it is fair to ask how it works in practice.
- **Referral network.** If you need a specialist, does the practice have a process for helping you find one?
- **Approach to shared decision-making.** How does the clinician involve patients in decisions? You can ask this directly, or look for how the practice describes its philosophy.

Avoid drawing conclusions from testimonials alone. Anecdotes can be informative, but they are not evidence, and they do not tell you how a practice will suit your particular situation.

## 3. Appointment format

DPC is often associated with longer appointments and different scheduling norms than conventional primary care. But formats vary, so compare specifics rather than assumptions.

Ask about:

- **Typical visit length** for new-patient and follow-up appointments.
- **Visit types** — in person, phone, video, or messaging — and which are included in the membership.
- **Scheduling lead time.** How far out are appointments typically booked?
- **Same-day or urgent needs.** What happens if you get sick and need to be seen quickly?
- **After-hours arrangements.** Who do you contact, and how?

If a practice advertises "unlimited access," it is reasonable to ask what that means concretely — for example, whether there are limits on message volume or visit frequency.

## 4. Accessibility

Accessibility covers both practical logistics and how easy the practice is to work with.

- **Location and hours.** Is the office reachable for you, and do the hours fit your schedule?
- **Communication channels.** Which channels does the practice use, and what is the expected response time?
- **Accessibility accommodations.** If you have mobility, sensory, or language needs, ask how the practice accommodates them.
- **Administrative friction.** How do you schedule, reschedule, and get records? How are questions handled between visits?

These details often determine whether a DPC relationship feels genuinely different from what you had before — or not.

## 5. Transparent pricing

Pricing is where clarity matters most, and where it pays to ask direct questions. This guide does not quote specific prices, because they vary and because a number without context can be misleading. Instead, focus on whether the practice is transparent about its structure.

Ask:

- **What exactly does the membership fee cover?** Which services are included, and which are not?
- **What is excluded?** Labs, procedures, medications, and specialist care are common areas where coverage differs.
- **Are there additional fees?** Ask about enrollment fees, annual fees, or charges for specific services.
- **How does the practice handle price changes?** What notice do patients receive?
- **How does this interact with insurance?** DPC arrangements are often described as separate from insurance, but the details matter for your situation. This is a good question for the practice and, if relevant, for a qualified professional familiar with your circumstances.
- **What are the terms for leaving?** Cancellation, refunds, and notice periods are worth understanding before you enroll.

Transparency itself is a signal. A practice that gives you clear, written answers to these questions is easier to evaluate than one that does not.

## A practical checklist

Use this as a starting point for your own notes. Add or remove items to fit your situation.

**Credentials**
- [ ] License and training background confirmed
- [ ] Board certification (if applicable) verified
- [ ] Care team roles understood
- [ ] Referral process explained

**Experience and fit**
- [ ] Scope of experience relevant to your needs
- [ ] Continuity expectations discussed
- [ ] Approach to shared decision-making understood

**Appointment format**
- [ ] Typical visit length known
- [ ] Visit types (in person, phone, video, messaging) clarified
- [ ] Scheduling lead time known
- [ ] Same-day and after-hours process explained

**Accessibility**
- [ ] Location and hours work for you
- [ ] Communication channels and response expectations clear
- [ ] Accommodations discussed if needed
- [ ] Administrative processes understood

**Pricing and terms**
- [ ] Membership fee and what it covers, in writing
- [ ] Exclusions identified
- [ ] Additional fees identified
- [ ] Price-change policy known
- [ ] Interaction with insurance clarified
- [ ] Cancellation and refund terms reviewed

## Questions to ask — and questions for a professional

Some questions are best directed to the practice itself: scheduling, fees, communication, and logistics. Others are situational and may benefit from input from a qualified professional — for example, how a membership arrangement fits with your insurance coverage, your budget, or your overall care plan.

This article is a decision framework, not individualized advice. It does not diagnose, treat, or promise outcomes, and it does not evaluate any specific provider. Suitability, evidence, risks, and alternatives are all worth discussing with a qualified professional who knows your circumstances.

## The bottom line

Choosing a DPC provider is a comparison exercise, not a single decision. Focus on credentials, relevant experience, appointment format, accessibility, and transparent pricing — and write down your own priorities before you start. Ask direct questions, get answers in writing where you can, and treat marketing language as a prompt for follow-up rather than a conclusion.

Finally, remember that this guide is general information. Please discuss your own situation with a qualified professional before making decisions about your care.
