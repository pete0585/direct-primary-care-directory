## Why preparation matters

A first conversation with a Direct Primary Care (DPC) practice is a decision-making meeting, not a commitment. You are gathering information to judge whether the arrangement fits your situation, your priorities, and the way you prefer to receive care. The more clearly you arrive with your own questions and context, the more useful the conversation tends to be.

This guide is a practical preparation tool. It is not clinical advice, not a treatment protocol, and not individualized legal or financial advice. It does not tell you what is right for you. Instead, it helps you organize your thinking and gives you a set of questions to raise with a qualified professional, so that the decisions you make rest on your own circumstances and on information you verify directly.

One useful starting point for thinking about how to choose any doctor or healthcare service is the MedlinePlus resource [Choosing a Doctor or Healthcare Service](https://medlineplus.gov/choosingadoctororhealthcareservice.html). It offers general orientation on selecting a clinician. You can read it alongside this checklist.

## What Direct Primary Care generally refers to

DPC is commonly described as a model of primary care in which a practice offers a direct relationship with patients, often structured around a membership or periodic fee rather than billing insurance for each visit. People researching DPC usually want to understand how appointments work, how communication happens between visits, what the membership covers, and how the practice interacts with insurance or other coverage.

Because practices vary, treat any general description, including this one, as a starting point for questions rather than a settled fact about a specific practice. The details that matter to you are the ones you confirm with the practice directly.

## Before the consultation: build your own picture

Preparation works best when it is concrete. Set aside time to write things down. A short, honest inventory of your situation will make the meeting more productive than any list of generic questions.

### 1. Clarify what you are trying to solve

Ask yourself:

- What prompted me to look into DPC now?
- What has felt frustrating or workable about my current primary care?
- What would a good outcome look like six months from now?
- Am I looking for a different style of relationship, different access, or both?

Writing these answers down keeps the conversation anchored to your actual needs rather than to marketing language.

### 2. Gather your practical context

Have the following ready, in whatever form you keep them:

- A list of your current medications and who prescribes them
- Names of specialists or other clinicians you see, and why
- Any upcoming tests, referrals, or follow-ups already in motion
- Your current insurance status and how you understand your coverage
- Any employer or benefit-program context that affects your options

You do not need to hand over documents at a first meeting. The point is to be able to answer questions accurately and to notice when something in the practice's description does not match your situation.

### 3. Decide what you are and are not willing to change

Some people are open to changing how they access care. Others want continuity with existing clinicians. Knowing your own boundaries in advance helps you evaluate what you hear rather than reacting in the moment.

## A practical checklist for the meeting

Use this as a working list. Not every item applies to everyone.

- [ ] Confirm how the practice describes its scope of primary care services
- [ ] Ask how appointments are scheduled and how quickly you can typically be seen
- [ ] Ask how communication between visits is handled and what response to expect
- [ ] Ask what is included in any membership or periodic fee and what is not
- [ ] Ask how the practice works with insurance, including any coverage you hold
- [ ] Ask how the practice handles referrals to specialists
- [ ] Ask how records are shared with other clinicians you see
- [ ] Ask what happens if you need care the practice does not provide
- [ ] Ask how the practice handles urgent concerns outside normal hours
- [ ] Ask what the process is if you decide the arrangement is not a fit
- [ ] Ask what questions the practice recommends you bring to your own insurer or benefits contact
- [ ] Ask for written materials you can review after the meeting

Treat checked boxes as notes for follow-up, not as conclusions. If an answer is vague, note that and ask again, or ask where you can read more.

## Questions to discuss with a qualified professional

The following questions are designed to be raised with the practice, with your insurer or benefits administrator, or with another qualified professional as appropriate. They are framed as questions because the answers depend on your situation and on details only the relevant professional can confirm.

### About suitability

- How would you describe the kind of patient this practice tends to serve well?
- What situations do you think this model may not suit, and why?
- Given what I have described about my needs, what would you want me to consider before deciding?
- Are there aspects of my situation that you would want another professional to weigh in on?

### About evidence and expectations

- What does the practice base its approach on, and where can I read more?
- What outcomes does the practice track, and how does it share that information?
- What should I not expect from this arrangement?
- How would we know, over time, whether this is working for me?

### About risks and trade-offs

- What are the practical trade-offs of this model compared with other ways of arranging primary care?
- What happens if my needs change or increase?
- How are disagreements or concerns handled?
- What are the limitations I should understand before deciding?

### About alternatives

- What other options would you suggest I compare this with?
- How would I evaluate those alternatives fairly?
- Are there questions I should ask any practice, not just this one?

These are conversation starters. They are not a substitute for advice from a qualified professional who knows your circumstances.

## How to evaluate what you hear

A few habits help you judge information without overreaching:

**Separate description from promise.** A practice can describe what it offers without guaranteeing an outcome. If you hear a claim about results, ask what it is based on and where you can read more.

**Notice what is left out.** Ask what the practice does not do. Clear boundaries are often more informative than broad assurances.

**Write down your impressions while they are fresh.** Note what felt clear, what felt evasive, and what you still need to check.

**Verify independently.** Confirm details with your insurer, your benefits contact, or another qualified professional rather than relying on a single conversation.

**Give yourself time.** A first consultation is one input. There is rarely a reason to decide on the spot.

## After the consultation

Once the meeting is over, review your notes against your original goals. Ask:

- Did I get clear answers to the questions that matter most to me?
- What is still unresolved, and who can help me resolve it?
- Does this arrangement fit my situation as I understand it today?
- What would I want to confirm in writing before deciding?

If you are comparing more than one practice, use the same checklist for each so your notes are comparable. If you are weighing this model against other ways of arranging primary care, keep the comparison grounded in your own priorities rather than in general impressions.
