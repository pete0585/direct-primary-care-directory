## Why service scope deserves its own comparison step

When people research Direct Primary Care (DPC), the conversation often starts with the membership model itself: a recurring fee, a direct relationship with a primary care practice, and a stated emphasis on accessibility. That framing is useful, but it can blur an important reality. Two practices that both describe themselves as Direct Primary Care may include meaningfully different services in their arrangements.

That is why service scope deserves its own comparison step. Scope is the practical answer to a simple question: what is actually included, and what sits outside the arrangement? If you compare only the general model and skip the specifics, you may choose an arrangement that fits your values but not your day-to-day needs.

This guide is about how to compare scope in a structured way. It is not a ranking of practices, and it does not describe any specific provider. It offers a repeatable method, a checklist you can bring to conversations, and hypothetical examples that are clearly labeled as examples. Nothing here is medical, legal, or financial advice. Where your situation involves clinical decisions, coverage rules, or contracts, consult qualified professionals.

## Start by separating three layers

Scope questions get easier when you separate them into three layers.

**Layer one: the core relationship.** This is the foundation of any DPC arrangement. It typically covers the ongoing primary care relationship itself, such as routine visits and communication with the practice. The details vary, so treat this layer as something to confirm rather than assume.

**Layer two: the included services.** This is where practices differ most. Included services might cover certain categories of visits, certain kinds of communication, or certain routine activities. The key word is *certain*. Scope documents usually define boundaries, and those boundaries are exactly what you want to understand.

**Layer three: the edges.** The edges are everything adjacent to the arrangement: services that are available but billed separately, services that are referred out, and services that are simply not part of the practice's offering. Edges are easy to overlook and often matter most when something unexpected comes up.

A useful habit is to ask about all three layers in the same conversation, in that order. It keeps the discussion concrete and prevents the general model from crowding out the specifics.

## A checklist for comparing service scope

Use the following checklist as a conversation guide. You do not need perfect answers to every item; you need enough clarity to compare arrangements on the same terms.

**1. What is the stated purpose of the arrangement?** Ask how the practice describes what the membership is for. A clear statement of purpose helps you interpret everything else.

**2. Which visit types are included?** Ask how included visits are described in writing. Look for categories rather than vague assurances.

**3. How is communication handled?** Ask which channels are part of the arrangement and how response expectations are described. Avoid assuming that any particular channel is included.

**4. What routine activities are covered?** Ask whether routine activities such as certain preventive conversations or follow-up discussions fall inside or outside the arrangement.

**5. What is explicitly excluded?** Ask directly. A practice that can describe its exclusions clearly is giving you useful information.

**6. What happens at the edges?** Ask how the practice handles situations that fall outside scope, including how it describes referrals or outside services.

**7. How is scope documented?** Ask where the scope is written down and whether you can review it before deciding.

**8. How are changes to scope communicated?** Ask how you would learn about changes over time.

**9. Who can answer scope questions?** Ask who at the practice is the right person for follow-up questions.

**10. What should you verify independently?** Ask what parts of the arrangement you should confirm with your own advisors, such as insurance, benefits, or legal questions.

This checklist is intentionally about questions rather than answers, because answers vary by practice and by your circumstances. The goal is a fair comparison, not a universal standard.

## Hypothetical examples (clearly labeled as examples)

The following scenarios are hypothetical. They are not descriptions of real practices, real people, or real outcomes. They exist only to show how the checklist can surface differences in scope.

**Example A: The clarity-first comparison.** Imagine a person comparing two DPC arrangements. Both describe a direct primary care relationship. When asked about included visits, the first arrangement provides a written description of categories, and the second provides only a general statement. Using the checklist, the person notes that the first arrangement gives them more to compare against. They do not conclude that one is better; they conclude that one is easier to evaluate. That distinction matters.

**Example B: The edge case.** Imagine someone who expects to need occasional coordination with specialists outside the practice. When they ask how the practice handles situations outside scope, they learn that the two arrangements describe this differently. One describes a process for referrals; the other describes it more loosely. The person uses this information to ask follow-up questions, not to make a snap judgment.

**Example C: The documentation question.** Imagine a person who wants to review scope in writing before deciding. They ask where scope is documented and whether they can review it in advance. One arrangement points them to a written description; the other prefers to explain it in conversation. The person weighs which approach fits how they prefer to make decisions.

**Example D: The change question.** Imagine someone planning a long-term relationship with a practice. They ask how changes to scope are communicated. The answers differ, and the person notes that this affects how predictable the arrangement feels over time.

In each example, the value comes from asking the same questions in the same order, then comparing the answers on equal footing.

## Turning answers into a comparison

Once you have answers, resist the urge to reduce them to a single score. Scope is multidimensional, and a simple ranking can hide the parts that matter to you.

A more useful approach is a short written summary for each arrangement you are considering. For each one, note what is included, what is excluded, how the edges are handled, and how scope is documented. Then note which items you could not get clear answers on. Unclear items are not automatically disqualifying, but they are worth tracking, because they tend to resurface later.

It also helps to separate what you know from what you assume. If an answer was implied rather than stated, mark it as an assumption. When you follow up, you can convert assumptions into answers.

## Questions to bring to a follow-up conversation

After a first pass, a short follow-up can resolve most remaining ambiguity. Consider asking:

- Can you point me to the written description of what is included?
- How would you describe what is outside the arrangement?
- What is the process when something falls outside scope?
- How would I learn about changes to scope over time?
- Who is the best person to ask if I have more questions?

These questions are deliberately open-ended. They invite description rather than yes-or-no answers, which tends to produce more useful information.

## A short recap

Service scope is the practical difference between a general DPC model and a specific arrangement. Compare it in three layers: the core relationship, the included services, and the edges. Use the same checklist for every practice so your comparison is fair. Treat hypothetical examples as illustrations of method, not as evidence about any real practice. And when a question touches on clinical care, coverage, or contracts, bring it to a qualified professional who can address your specific situation.

The goal is not to find a perfect arrangement. It is to understand what you are choosing, in your own words, before you decide.
