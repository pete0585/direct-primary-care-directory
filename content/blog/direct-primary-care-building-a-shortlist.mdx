## Start with a clear picture of what you actually need

Direct Primary Care (DPC) is a model where patients typically pay a recurring membership fee directly to a primary care practice for a defined set of services. That is the general idea, but the details vary widely from practice to practice. Because of that variation, the most useful first step is not comparing practices. It is clarifying your own needs.

Consider three areas:

1. **Access and availability.** How quickly do you want to be able to reach someone? Do you value same-day or next-day appointments, or is a scheduled visit a week out acceptable? Do you prefer in-person visits, remote visits, or a mix? Think about your work schedule, caregiving responsibilities, and how far you are willing to travel.
2. **Scope of services.** DPC practices commonly describe services such as routine primary care, preventive care, chronic condition management, and care coordination. But what is included and what is not can differ. Some practices may offer additional services like certain in-office procedures, while others may not. Ask for a written list.
3. **Communication style.** Do you want a provider who is highly responsive by message or phone? Do you prefer a more structured visit schedule? There is no single right answer, but knowing your preference will help you evaluate fit.

Write these needs down. A short, honest list is more useful than a long wish list.

## Build a candidate list from multiple sources

Once you know what you need, gather options. A few practical sources:

- **Directories.** DPC-focused directories can help you see practices in your area. Look for listings that describe services, membership structure, and location.
- **Professional referrals.** Ask your current primary care clinician, a specialist you see, or a trusted nurse or pharmacist if they are aware of DPC practices locally. They may not be able to recommend one, but they can often point you toward options.
- **Community input.** Friends, family, and local community groups can share experiences. Treat these as starting points, not endorsements.
- **Practice websites.** A practice website can tell you how they describe their model, what services they list, and how to contact them.

Aim for a candidate list of roughly three to six practices. More than that can become unwieldy; fewer than three may not give you enough comparison.

## Use a comparison checklist

A checklist helps you compare practices on the same dimensions. Here is a practical one you can adapt.

### 1. Services and scope
- What services are explicitly included in the membership?
- What services are excluded or billed separately?
- How are referrals to specialists handled?
- Are there any limitations on visit frequency or communication channels?

### 2. Access and communication
- How do you contact the practice for routine questions?
- What are the typical response times?
- Are remote visits offered? Under what circumstances?
- What are the hours of operation, including after-hours options?

### 3. Membership structure
- What is the membership term (monthly, annual)?
- Are there enrollment fees or other one-time costs?
- What is the process for pausing or canceling a membership?
- Does the practice accept health insurance for any services, or is it entirely direct-pay?

### 4. Provider and practice fit
- Who will you primarily see? Is it a specific clinician or a team?
- What is the clinician's background and approach to care?
- How does the practice handle urgent concerns that fall outside routine care?
- What is the practice's approach to coordinating with other clinicians you see?

### 5. Practical logistics
- Location and parking or transit access.
- Appointment scheduling process.
- How records are shared with you and with other providers.
- Any technology requirements for remote visits or messaging.

Use the same checklist for each practice. Note where information is unclear, and plan to ask about it.

## Hypothetical examples (clearly labeled as examples)

These examples are invented for illustration only. They are not real practices, and they are not recommendations.

**Example A: The commuter who values remote access.**
A hypothetical patient works long hours and travels frequently. They prioritize a practice that offers remote visits and quick message responses. On their checklist, they mark access and communication as highest priority. They shortlist two practices that describe remote options and ask specific questions about response times and how remote visits are scheduled. They deprioritize practices that only offer in-person visits during standard business hours.

**Example B: The parent managing a chronic condition.**
A hypothetical patient manages a chronic condition and wants a clinician who can coordinate with specialists. They prioritize scope of services and care coordination. They ask each practice how they handle referrals, how they share records, and what happens if a specialist needs to be involved. They shortlist practices that describe a team-based approach and clear coordination processes.

**Example C: The person who wants a simple, predictable structure.**
A hypothetical patient wants to understand exactly what is included and what is not. They prioritize membership structure and transparency. They ask for a written list of included services and a clear explanation of any additional costs. They shortlist practices that provide detailed written information and answer questions directly.

In each example, the shortlist follows from the person's stated needs, not from a ranking of practices.

## Questions to ask during a shortlist conversation

When you contact a practice, you are gathering information, not committing. A few questions that can surface important details:

- Can you walk me through what is included in the membership?
- How do you handle questions between visits?
- What happens if I need care that is outside your scope?
- How do you coordinate with specialists or other clinicians?
- What is the process if I need to cancel or pause my membership?
- Who will I typically see, and what is their approach?
- Are there any services that are not included but commonly needed?

Take notes. Compare answers side by side. If a practice is vague or unwilling to answer, that is useful information.

## Narrowing to a shortlist

After gathering information, review your checklist and your notes. A few practical steps:

1. **Eliminate clear mismatches.** If a practice does not offer a service you need, or if logistics make it impractical, remove it.
2. **Rank remaining practices by your top priorities.** If access is your top priority, weigh that heavily. If coordination is more important, weigh that instead.
3. **Identify open questions.** For your top two or three practices, list what you still need to know. Ask those questions before deciding.
4. **Consider a trial or initial visit.** Some practices may offer an introductory conversation or visit. If so, use it to assess fit.
5. **Trust your judgment.** Fit matters. If a practice feels like a poor match in communication style, that is a legitimate consideration.

## What to avoid

- **Assuming all DPC practices are the same.** They are not. Services, access, and structure vary.
- **Relying on a single source.** Use multiple sources to build your candidate list.
- **Skipping the questions.** A shortlist is only useful if you have enough information to compare.
- **Feeling pressured to decide quickly.** Take the time you need.

## When to consult a professional

This guide is for general information only. It is not medical, legal, or financial advice. If you have specific health concerns, talk with a qualified clinician. If you have questions about insurance, taxes, or contracts, consult an appropriate professional. DPC membership agreements can vary, so read them carefully and ask for clarification on anything you do not understand.

## A final note

Building a shortlist is a process of matching your needs to what practices actually offer. A clear checklist, a few targeted questions, and a willingness to compare can help you make a more informed decision. You do not need to find the perfect practice on the first try. You need enough information to choose well.
