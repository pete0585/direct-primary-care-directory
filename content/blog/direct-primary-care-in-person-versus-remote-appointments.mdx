# Direct Primary Care: In-person versus remote appointments: questions to ask

If you are researching Direct Primary Care (DPC), you have probably noticed that many practices offer both in-person and remote visits. That flexibility can be genuinely useful, but it also raises a practical question: for any given concern, which format actually fits? The honest answer is that it depends on the practice, the situation, and what you are trying to accomplish. This guide is written to help you ask better questions during the research phase, not to tell you what your care should look like.

Nothing here is medical advice, and nothing here replaces a conversation with a qualified professional. Think of this as a decision framework you can bring to a consultation.

## Why the format question matters in DPC

In many traditional settings, the visit format is largely decided for you by scheduling constraints. DPC arrangements often emphasize longer visits, direct access, and a defined scope of services, which can make format a more open conversation. That openness is a benefit, but it also means you may need to clarify expectations up front.

A few broad considerations tend to come up:

- **What can reasonably be handled remotely?** Practices differ in how they draw this line.
- **What requires a physical presence?** Some evaluations involve touch, observation, or equipment.
- **How is continuity maintained?** The value of a visit often depends on what happens before and after it.
- **What are the logistics?** Scheduling, response times, and follow-up pathways vary.

Your job during research is not to memorize clinical rules. It is to understand how a given practice operates so you can make an informed choice.

## A checklist of questions to ask

Use these as prompts. You do not need to ask all of them, and you can adapt the wording.

### Scope and availability

1. Which types of concerns does the practice typically handle in person, and which does it typically handle remotely?
2. Are there situations where the practice would ask me to come in even if I requested a remote visit?
3. Are there situations where the practice would suggest remote when I expected in person?
4. How does the practice decide, and who makes that call?
5. Does the answer change for new patients versus established patients?

### Access and scheduling

6. How do I request an in-person visit versus a remote visit?
7. What is the typical wait for each format?
8. Are there same-day options, and in which format?
9. Can I switch formats after scheduling, and how far in advance?
10. What happens if I need to be seen and my preferred format is unavailable?

### The remote experience

11. What platform or method is used for remote visits?
12. What do I need on my end (device, connection, private space)?
13. How is my information handled during and after a remote visit?
14. What should I do if the connection fails partway through?
15. Is there a way to share information before the visit so time is used well?

### The in-person experience

16. What is the typical visit length for in-person appointments?
17. What can be done during an in-person visit that is not typically done remotely?
18. How is the office set up for privacy and comfort?
19. How are follow-up steps communicated after an in-person visit?
20. Are there accessibility considerations I should ask about?

### Continuity and follow-up

21. How are notes and next steps shared after either format?
22. Who do I contact with questions between visits, and how?
23. How does the practice coordinate with other professionals I may see?
24. If a concern needs a different setting, how is that conversation handled?
25. How does the practice keep track of preferences I express about format?

### Membership and expectations

26. Does the membership arrangement describe what is included for each format?
27. Are there any format-specific considerations I should understand before joining?
28. How are changes to services or policies communicated?
29. What questions does the practice wish more people asked?
30. Who is the best person to contact with follow-up questions?

## Hypothetical examples (clearly labeled as examples)

The following scenarios are invented illustrations. They are not drawn from real patients, real practices, or real outcomes, and they are not clinical recommendations.

**Example A — A scheduling question.**
Suppose a person is comparing two DPC practices. Practice One offers remote visits for most follow-ups and reserves in-person slots for a narrower set of situations. Practice Two encourages in-person visits for nearly everything and offers remote mainly for brief check-ins. Neither approach is inherently better. The useful move is to ask each practice how it decides, then compare those answers against your own preferences and circumstances.

**Example B — A format mismatch.**
Imagine someone books a remote visit, then learns partway through that the practice would prefer an in-person evaluation for that particular concern. A well-prepared person would have asked in advance: "If you decide in-person is better, how do we handle that, and is there any impact on scheduling or cost?" The answer may be simple, but knowing it beforehand reduces friction.

**Example C — A continuity question.**
Consider a person who has a remote visit and then wonders how follow-up works. They might ask: "After a remote visit, how do I get my next steps, and who do I contact if something changes?" The answer reveals a lot about how a practice communicates.

**Example D — A preference to document.**
Suppose someone strongly prefers in-person visits for most things but values remote access for convenience. They could ask: "Can I note a preference in my record, and how is that handled when scheduling?" This is a process question, not a clinical one.

In each example, the goal is the same: replace assumptions with specific answers.

## How to weigh the answers

Once you have responses, you might organize them into a few buckets:

- **Clarity.** Did the practice give direct answers, or did you leave with more questions?
- **Fit.** Do the default patterns match how you like to engage with a care team?
- **Flexibility.** How easily can you move between formats when your needs change?
- **Communication.** Is it obvious who to contact, how, and when?
- **Consistency.** Do the answers you received line up with written materials you were given?

None of these buckets tells you what to decide. They simply help you compare options in a structured way.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Assuming remote is always faster.** Sometimes it is; sometimes in-person scheduling is more available. Ask.
- **Assuming in-person is always necessary.** Practices vary widely. Ask how they decide.
- **Treating one answer as universal.** Policies can differ by situation. Ask about the general pattern and the exceptions.
- **Overlooking follow-up.** The format of a visit is only part of the picture. Ask what happens afterward.
- **Skipping the paperwork.** Written materials may answer questions you were about to ask. Read them, then ask about anything unclear.

## A short pre-decision checklist

Before you settle on a practice, confirm that you can answer these:

- [ ] I know which concerns the practice typically handles in person.
- [ ] I know which concerns the practice typically handles remotely.
- [ ] I know how to request each format.
- [ ] I know typical wait times for each format.
- [ ] I know how to switch formats if needed.
- [ ] I know what I need for a remote visit.
- [ ] I know what to expect during an in-person visit.
- [ ] I know how follow-up works after either format.
- [ ] I know who to contact with questions between visits.
- [ ] I know how my preferences are recorded and used.

If any box is unchecked, that is a good signal to ask one more question before deciding.

## When to involve a qualified professional

Questions about your own health, what type of evaluation is appropriate for a specific concern, and how to interpret any information you receive are best directed to a qualified professional who knows your situation. This article is about how to research and compare DPC service formats, not about diagnosing or treating anything. If you are unsure whether a concern needs attention, that is a conversation to have with a clinician, not a decision to make from a blog post.

## The bottom line

The in-person versus remote question in Direct Primary Care is less about finding a single right answer and more about understanding how a specific practice operates. Ask direct questions, listen for specifics, and compare the answers against your own preferences. A practice that welcomes your questions and answers them clearly is giving you useful information about how it communicates.

Use the checklist above as a starting point, adapt it to your situation, and consult qualified professionals for anything related to your own care.
