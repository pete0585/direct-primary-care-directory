## Why fee clarity matters before you commit

Direct Primary Care (DPC) is often described as a membership-style approach to primary care: you pay a recurring fee directly to a practice, and in exchange you get defined access to a primary care clinician and a set of included services. Because DPC sits outside the usual insurance billing path for routine primary care, the fee structure is central to the relationship. That is also why fee confusion is one of the most common sources of frustration for people researching DPC.

This guide is about getting clarity, not about telling you what a fair fee is. Fees vary by practice, region, and the scope of what is included, and no single number or model is right for everyone. The goal here is to help you walk into a conversation with a DPC practice able to ask precise questions, read a fee schedule critically, and decide whether the arrangement fits your situation.

This article offers general education only. It is not individualized medical, legal, tax, or financial advice. For decisions about your own care, coverage, or finances, consult qualified professionals.

## Start with the shape of the fee, not the number

Many people begin by comparing monthly prices. That is understandable, but it often leads to apples-to-oranges comparisons. Before you compare amounts, get clear on the *structure* of the fee. Common structural questions include:

- Is the fee a flat monthly amount, or does it vary by age, household size, or another factor?
- Is there an enrollment or onboarding fee separate from the recurring fee?
- Is there a minimum commitment period, and what happens if you leave early?
- Are there different tiers, and what specifically changes between them?
- Are there any per-visit charges, or is the visit itself included?
- How and when are fees collected, and what payment methods are accepted?

Two practices can both advertise "a monthly membership" and still have very different structures. Getting the structure straight first makes the number meaningful.

## The fee-clarity checklist

Use this checklist as a note-taking tool during a consultation or while reading a practice's written materials. It is designed to surface the details that are easy to overlook.

**1. What is included in the recurring fee?**
Ask for a written list of included services. Look for whether the list covers visits, communication between visits, and any preventive services the practice offers. If something is described vaguely, ask for a concrete example.

**2. What is explicitly not included?**
A clear practice should be able to tell you what falls outside the membership. Common categories to ask about include laboratory work, imaging, medications, procedures, and referrals to specialists. You do not need to memorize every exclusion, but you should understand the major buckets.

**3. How are outside costs handled?**
If a service is not included, how would it be billed? Would it go through insurance, be paid directly, or be arranged another way? Ask who you would pay and roughly how you would find out the cost before agreeing to something.

**4. What happens if you need care the practice does not provide?**
Ask how the practice coordinates with specialists, urgent care, or hospitals. The answer helps you understand the boundaries of the membership.

**5. What are the terms for pausing or ending the membership?**
Ask about notice periods, refunds, and any conditions. Get this in writing if possible.

**6. Who do you contact with billing questions?**
A named point of contact, or at least a clear process, reduces the chance of surprises later.

**7. What does the practice expect from you?**
Some practices have expectations around scheduling, communication channels, or payment timing. Knowing these upfront prevents misunderstandings.

## Questions that tend to produce useful answers

Open-ended questions often reveal more than yes-or-no ones. Consider asking:

- "Could you walk me through what a typical month looks like for a member, in terms of what is covered and what is not?"
- "If I needed a service that is not included, how would I find out what it would cost before I agree to it?"
- "What questions do new members most often have about fees, and how do you usually answer them?"
- "Is there anything about the fee structure that members sometimes find surprising?"
- "Could you show me where the fee terms are written down?"

That last question is important. A practice that can point you to written terms is easier to evaluate than one that only describes fees verbally.

## Clearly labeled hypothetical examples

The following scenarios are illustrative examples only. They are not descriptions of real practices, real prices, or real outcomes, and they are not recommendations.

**Example A: The flat-fee practice.**
Imagine a practice that charges one monthly amount regardless of how often you visit, with a written list of included services and a separate list of services that are not included. A person comparing this to another practice would want to know whether the included list matches the care they actually expect to use, and how the practice handles anything outside the list.

**Example B: The tiered practice.**
Imagine a practice with two membership tiers. The lower tier includes visits and messaging; the higher tier adds additional services. A person evaluating this would want the practice to explain, in plain language, what changes between tiers and whether they can move between them.

**Example C: The practice with an enrollment fee.**
Imagine a practice that charges a one-time enrollment fee plus a recurring monthly fee, with a minimum commitment period. A person considering this would want to understand the total cost over the commitment period, the notice required to leave, and whether any portion is refundable.

In each example, the useful move is the same: ask for the written terms, identify what is included and excluded, and confirm the exit conditions before committing.

## Reading a fee schedule critically

When you receive a fee schedule or membership agreement, read it with a few habits in mind:

- **Look for definitions.** Terms like "visit," "consultation," and "care coordination" can mean different things in different practices. If a term is doing a lot of work in the document, ask how the practice defines it.
- **Separate recurring from one-time costs.** A low monthly fee paired with significant one-time costs can look different than it first appears.
- **Note anything described as "may" or "at the practice's discretion."** These phrases are not automatically problems, but they are worth clarifying.
- **Check the effective date.** Fee schedules can change. Ask how members are notified of changes and how much advance notice they receive.
- **Ask about your own situation.** If you have a condition or a pattern of care you expect to use, ask how the fee structure would apply. The practice can speak to its own policies; your clinician can speak to your care.

## Common sources of fee confusion

A few patterns come up often enough to be worth naming:

- **Assuming "membership" means "everything."** Membership usually means a defined set of services, not unlimited access to all of medicine.
- **Confusing the DPC fee with insurance.** The DPC fee and any insurance premium are separate. How they interact depends on your coverage and the practice's policies.
- **Overlooking household pricing.** Some practices price per person, others per household. The difference matters if you are evaluating for a family.
- **Missing the communication policy.** If messaging or after-hours contact is part of what you value, confirm how it is included and any limits.
- **Forgetting to ask about changes.** Fees and included services can be updated over time. Knowing the process in advance helps you plan.

## A short pre-commitment recap

Before you decide, it can help to write down your own answers to these questions:

1. What do I expect to use most often, and is it included?
2. What are the most likely services I would need outside the membership, and how would those be handled?
3. What is the total cost over the commitment period, including any one-time fees?
4. What are the terms for changing or ending the membership?
5. Where are the written terms, and do I understand them?

If you cannot answer these, that is a signal to ask more questions rather than to guess.

## A final note on judgment

There is no universal "right" DPC fee, and a higher or lower number does not by itself tell you whether a practice is a good fit. What matters is whether the structure is transparent, whether the included services match what you expect to use, and whether the terms are ones you can live with. If a practice is unwilling or unable to explain its fees clearly, that is useful information in itself.

Take your time, ask your questions in writing where you can, and consult qualified professionals for advice specific to your health, coverage, and finances. Clarity about fees is not a guarantee about care, but it is a reasonable place to start.
