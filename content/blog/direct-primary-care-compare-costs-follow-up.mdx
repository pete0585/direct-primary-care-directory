# Direct Primary Care: How to Compare Costs Without Overlooking Follow-Up

Choosing a Direct Primary Care (DPC) provider often starts with a simple question: what does the membership cost? That question matters, but it is rarely the whole picture. Two memberships with similar monthly fees can feel very different in practice once you factor in what happens after the first visit — how follow-up works, how quickly you can get questions answered, and whether the relationship is built for continuity or for one-off appointments.

This guide is a practical decision framework. It focuses on how to compare costs without losing sight of follow-up. It does not recommend a specific provider, does not quote real prices, and does not offer medical or legal advice. Where your situation involves clinical decisions, coverage questions, or contracts, consult qualified professionals.

## Why follow-up belongs in the cost conversation

In a fee-for-service model, each visit tends to generate a separate charge. In a DPC membership, you typically pay a recurring fee for access to a provider's panel, and the value of that fee depends heavily on how the relationship works between visits.

If you compare only the headline monthly number, you may miss:

- How many follow-up contacts are included, and what counts as a follow-up.
- Whether messaging, phone calls, or telehealth check-ins are part of the membership.
- How quickly you can expect a response, and during what hours.
- Whether the provider has capacity to see you again soon when something changes.
- What happens if you need care outside the DPC relationship.

A lower monthly fee with limited follow-up access can end up feeling more expensive in time, frustration, and out-of-pocket costs than a higher fee with strong continuity. The goal is not to find the cheapest membership; it is to find the membership whose total value matches how you actually use care.

## A cost comparison framework

Instead of comparing a single number, compare across four layers.

### Layer 1: The membership itself

Start with the basics you can verify from public materials:

- Monthly or annual membership fee.
- Enrollment or onboarding fee, if any.
- Household or family pricing structure.
- Age-based pricing tiers, if applicable.
- Notice period or commitment terms.

Write these down in a simple table so you are comparing like with like. If a provider does not publish a number, note that as an open question rather than assuming.

### Layer 2: What is included between visits

This is where follow-up lives. Ask specifically:

- Are messages to the provider included, and is there a limit?
- Are phone or video check-ins included?
- Are follow-up visits after an initial visit included, or billed separately?
- Are routine preventive visits part of the membership?
- Are there services that are explicitly excluded?

A membership that includes generous between-visit contact may reduce the number of separate visits you need. A membership that does not may shift more of your care into per-visit or external costs.

### Layer 3: What sits outside the membership

No DPC membership covers everything. Common categories outside the membership can include:

- Laboratory work and imaging.
- Medications and prescriptions.
- Specialist visits.
- Emergency or hospital care.
- Procedures performed outside the DPC setting.

You do not need exact figures to compare. You need to know which categories exist so you can estimate your own exposure. If you have insurance, check how it interacts with a DPC membership. If you do not, note that DPC is not a substitute for coverage against major medical events.

### Layer 4: Access and continuity

This layer is harder to price but easy to feel. Consider:

- Typical response time for non-urgent questions.
- Same-day or next-day availability.
- How the provider handles care when they are unavailable.
- Whether you would see the same provider consistently.
- How the provider coordinates with specialists or outside clinicians.

Continuity is often the reason people choose DPC in the first place. If follow-up is slow or fragmented, the membership may not deliver the experience you are paying for.

## A practical checklist

Use this checklist when reviewing a provider's public information or preparing questions for an introductory conversation.

**Membership and cost**
- [ ] Monthly or annual fee is stated clearly.
- [ ] Enrollment fee, if any, is disclosed.
- [ ] Family or household pricing is explained.
- [ ] Cancellation and notice terms are available.

**Follow-up and access**
- [ ] Messaging policy is described.
- [ ] Phone or video check-ins are addressed.
- [ ] Follow-up visit policy is stated.
- [ ] Expected response times are described.
- [ ] After-hours or urgent guidance is explained.

**Scope of services**
- [ ] Included services are listed.
- [ ] Excluded services are listed.
- [ ] Referral and coordination approach is described.
- [ ] Relationship to insurance is explained.

**Fit and logistics**
- [ ] Location and hours work for you.
- [ ] Panel size or availability is discussed.
- [ ] Provider background and scope are described.
- [ ] You know how to raise concerns if something is unclear.

If several boxes are blank, that is useful information. It may mean the provider has not published details, or it may mean you need to ask directly.

## Hypothetical examples (illustrative only)

The following examples are invented to show how the framework works. They are not real providers, prices, or outcomes.

**Example A: Low fee, thin follow-up**

A hypothetical membership advertises a low monthly fee. The public materials mention unlimited visits but do not describe messaging or phone support. Follow-up visits are available but scheduling is described as "as capacity allows." For someone who mostly needs occasional routine visits, this might be workable. For someone managing an ongoing concern that needs frequent check-ins, the low fee may not translate into low total effort or cost.

**Example B: Higher fee, strong continuity**

A hypothetical membership has a higher monthly fee and explicitly includes messaging, phone check-ins, and follow-up visits within the membership. The provider describes a limited panel and same-day or next-day access for established members. For someone who values quick answers and continuity, the higher fee may reflect real value — but it still needs to fit the budget and the rest of the care picture.

**Example C: Similar fees, different structures**

Two hypothetical memberships list similar monthly fees. One includes preventive visits but bills follow-up visits separately. The other includes follow-up visits but charges for certain preventive services. Neither is automatically better. The right choice depends on which services you expect to use and how you prefer to pay for them.

In each example, the lesson is the same: compare the structure, not just the number.

## Questions worth asking directly

When you speak with a provider or their staff, these questions tend to surface the details that matter:

1. What is included in the membership, and what is billed separately?
2. How do follow-up visits work after an initial visit?
3. How do members typically reach you between visits, and what response time should they expect?
4. What happens when you are unavailable or on leave?
5. How do you coordinate with specialists or outside clinicians?
6. How does the membership interact with insurance, if I have it?
7. What are the terms for pausing or ending the membership?

You do not need perfect answers to every question. You need enough clarity to make an informed decision and to know what you are and are not buying.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Comparing only the monthly fee.** Follow-up access, included services, and exclusions change the real cost.
- **Assuming "unlimited" means unlimited everything.** Check what the term covers.
- **Ignoring what happens outside the membership.** Labs, imaging, medications, specialists, and emergencies still exist.
- **Skipping the contract details.** Notice periods and cancellation terms matter if your situation changes.
- **Treating DPC as a substitute for insurance.** DPC is a care model, not coverage against major medical costs.

## Bottom line

Comparing Direct Primary Care costs well means comparing structure, not just price. Put the membership fee, the follow-up policy, the excluded services, and the access model side by side. Use the checklist, ask the direct questions, and weigh the total value against how you actually use care. A membership that fits your follow-up needs is often worth more than one that only looks cheaper on paper.
