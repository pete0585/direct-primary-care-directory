# Direct Primary Care: How to Prepare a Useful Question List

Choosing a Direct Primary Care (DPC) provider is a decision that works best when you go in prepared. A DPC arrangement typically centers on a direct relationship between you and a primary care practice, often structured around a membership or retainer-style model rather than visit-by-visit billing through insurance. Because the model can look and feel different from a conventional primary care office, the questions you bring to an initial conversation matter a great deal.

This guide is about preparation. It will help you build a question list that is specific, useful, and grounded in what you actually need. It does not recommend a particular provider, does not promise clinical outcomes, and does not offer individualized medical or legal advice. Where a question touches on your personal health situation, insurance coverage, or legal rights, please consult a qualified professional.

## Why a Question List Beats Improvisation

Most people arrive at a first DPC conversation with a general sense of what they want: easier access, more time with a clinician, or a clearer relationship. Those instincts are a good starting point, but they are not yet a plan. A prepared question list does three things:

1. **It surfaces the practical details.** Scope of services, how contact works, and what happens when you need care outside the practice are all things you can ask about directly.
2. **It reduces guesswork.** Instead of assuming what a practice offers, you can ask and compare answers across providers.
3. **It keeps the conversation focused.** A written list helps you cover what matters to you even if the meeting is short.

A useful question list is not a script you read aloud. It is a reference you keep nearby, organized so you can find the right question at the right moment.

## Start With Your Own Situation

Before you write a single question for a provider, write a few notes for yourself. This step is easy to skip and hard to replace.

- **What prompted you to look at DPC now?** A recent experience, a change in coverage, or a desire for a different kind of relationship with a primary care practice are all valid starting points.
- **What does a good week of care look like for you?** Think about how you would prefer to reach someone, how quickly you hope to hear back, and what kind of visits feel manageable.
- **What are your non-negotiables?** These might relate to location, hours, or how a practice communicates. Naming them now makes it easier to notice a mismatch later.
- **What are you unsure about?** Uncertainty is not a weakness in this process. It is a signal about where to aim your questions.

Your notes do not need to be polished. A few lines are enough to anchor the rest of your preparation.

## A Checklist for Building Your Question List

Use this checklist as a structure. You do not need every item, and you can add your own. The goal is coverage, not completeness.

### Access and communication

- How do I contact the practice for routine questions?
- How do I contact the practice for something urgent?
- What are the typical response times, and do they differ by channel?
- Is there a patient portal, and how is it used?
- Who answers when I reach out, and how is that decided?

### Scope of services

- What services are included in the arrangement?
- What services are outside the arrangement?
- How are referrals handled when I need care beyond primary care?
- What happens if I need care while traveling or away from the area?

### Scheduling and availability

- How are appointments scheduled?
- What do same-day or next-day options look like, if any?
- Are there evening or weekend options?
- How far in advance do routine visits tend to be booked?

### The relationship itself

- How would you describe the working relationship between patient and practice?
- How are care decisions discussed?
- How do you handle disagreements or changes in direction?
- What does continuity look like over time?

### Practical and administrative

- What does the enrollment process involve?
- What should I read before deciding?
- How do I ask about changes to the arrangement later?
- Who do I contact with administrative questions?

### Boundaries and expectations

- What is the practice not able to do?
- When would you recommend I look elsewhere for care?
- How do you handle situations that fall outside your scope?

That last section is often the most valuable. A practice that can speak clearly about its own boundaries is giving you real information, not a sales pitch.

## Turning the Checklist Into Real Questions

A checklist of topics is not yet a question list. The difference is specificity. Vague questions produce vague answers, and vague answers are hard to compare.

Consider the difference between these two approaches:

- **General:** "How does communication work?"
- **Specific:** "If I send a routine question through the portal on a Tuesday morning, when should I expect a reply, and who replies?"

The second version gives the practice something concrete to respond to, and it gives you something concrete to compare across providers.

Here is a small set of example questions, written in that more specific style. These are hypothetical examples, offered to illustrate the format rather than to describe any real practice.

> **Example (hypothetical):** "If I have a question that is not urgent but not routine either, what is the recommended way to reach the practice, and what response time should I plan for?"
>
> **Example (hypothetical):** "If I need a specialist, how does the practice typically help me find one, and what does that process look like from my side?"
>
> **Example (hypothetical):** "If my needs change over time, how would we revisit whether this arrangement is still a good fit?"

Notice that none of these examples ask for a promise. They ask for a description. Descriptions are what you can compare.

## Organizing Your List So You Actually Use It

A long list is not automatically a good list. Organize yours so it works in a real conversation.

- **Put your top five questions first.** If the meeting is short, you still cover what matters most.
- **Group related questions together.** Access questions belong with access questions, scope with scope.
- **Leave room for follow-ups.** Write down what you hear and note where you want to go deeper.
- **Mark questions as must-ask, nice-to-ask, or later.** Not everything needs an answer on day one.
- **Keep a version for each provider.** If you speak with more than one practice, the same list lets you compare answers side by side.

A simple table or two-column note works well: question on the left, what you heard on the right.

## Adapting the List to Your Priorities

Different people need different lists. The structure above is a starting point, not a rule.

If your priority is **access**, spend more of your list on communication channels, response expectations, and scheduling.

If your priority is **continuity**, spend more of your list on how the relationship is described, how care decisions are discussed, and what happens over time.

If your priority is **clarity about what is included**, spend more of your list on scope, boundaries, and what falls outside the arrangement.

If your priority is **practical fit**, spend more of your list on location, hours, enrollment steps, and administrative contact.

You can combine these. Most people do. The point is to let your own priorities shape which questions you ask first.

## What to Do With the Answers

A question list is only useful if you do something with the responses. After a conversation, take a few minutes to review your notes while they are fresh.

- **Note what was clear.** Clear answers are a good sign that the practice communicates well.
- **Note what was vague.** Vague answers are not necessarily a problem, but they are worth a follow-up question.
- **Note what you did not ask.** It is normal to leave a conversation with new questions. Add them to your list.
- **Note how you felt.** Comfort and trust are part of the decision, even if they are hard to measure.

If you are comparing multiple providers, resist the urge to rank them on a single dimension. A practice that answers one question well may answer another less clearly. Look at the overall picture.

## Common Preparation Mistakes

A few patterns tend to make this process harder than it needs to be.

- **Waiting to prepare until the meeting starts.** Even ten minutes of preparation makes a difference.
- **Asking only about what is included.** What is outside the arrangement matters just as much.
- **Treating the first conversation as a commitment.** It is a conversation. You can take your time.
- **Assuming all practices work the same way.** Direct Primary Care practices can differ in meaningful ways, which is exactly why asking is worthwhile.
- **Leaving with unanswered questions you did not write down.** Your list is the safeguard against that.

## A Short Pre-Meeting Routine

The day before a conversation, try this routine:

1. **Read your notes about your own situation.** Remind yourself what prompted this search.
2. **Review your top five questions.** Say them out loud once so they feel natural.
3. **Check your logistics.** Confirm the time, the format, and anything you need to bring.
4. **Set one goal for the conversation.** For example: "I want to understand how communication works and what is outside the arrangement."
5. **Leave space.** A good conversation will raise new questions, and that is a sign it is working.

## A Final Note on Judgment

No question list can answer everything, and no single conversation will tell you whether a practice is right for you. What a good list does is give you a fair basis for your own judgment. It helps you compare descriptions rather than impressions, and it helps you notice when something important is still unclear.

Take your time. Ask the questions that matter to you. And where your situation involves your health, your coverage, or your legal rights, bring those questions to a qualified professional who can speak to your specific circumstances.
