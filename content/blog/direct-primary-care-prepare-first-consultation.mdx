# Direct Primary Care: How to Prepare for a First Consultation

Choosing a Direct Primary Care (DPC) provider is a decision about a relationship, not just a transaction. A first consultation is your chance to see how a provider communicates, whether the practice's structure matches your needs, and whether you feel comfortable asking questions. This guide is written for people who are researching DPC services and want to walk into that first conversation prepared. It is not medical, legal, or financial advice, and it does not describe any specific provider. Where your personal health, insurance, or legal situation is involved, please consult a qualified professional.

## What a Direct Primary Care First Consultation Usually Is

A DPC first consultation is typically an introductory conversation. It may be in person, by phone, or by video. The purpose is usually mutual: you learn about the practice, and the practice learns about you. It is often not a full clinical visit, and it is usually not the moment you receive a diagnosis or a treatment plan. Think of it as a structured meet-and-greet with practical questions attached.

Because DPC practices vary, the specifics of what happens in a first consultation can differ. Some practices may offer a short introductory meeting at no charge, while others may treat the first visit as a standard consultation. Because details like this vary and can change, confirm them directly with the practice rather than assuming.

## Why Preparation Matters

A prepared first consultation tends to be more useful. When you arrive with your questions already written down, you are less likely to forget the things that matter most to you. You also give the provider a clearer picture of what you are looking for, which can make the conversation more productive.

Preparation also helps you compare practices fairly. If you ask the same core questions of several providers, you can compare their answers side by side instead of relying on a general impression.

## Before the Consultation: A Practical Checklist

Use this checklist as a starting point. Adapt it to your situation.

- **Clarify your own goals.** Write down what you want from a primary care relationship. Examples might include same-day access for urgent concerns, longer visit times, a provider who knows your history, or support for a specific ongoing condition. These are examples of goals, not promises about what any practice offers.
- **List your current medications and allergies.** Even if the first consultation is not clinical, having this information ready is useful and shows the provider you are organized.
- **Gather your health history summary.** A short written summary of major conditions, surgeries, and family history can help the conversation.
- **Note your insurance situation.** DPC practices often structure payment differently from insurance-based practices. Know whether you have insurance, what it covers, and how you intend to use it alongside DPC.
- **Write your top five questions.** Keep them short and specific.
- **Check logistics.** Confirm the address, parking, telehealth link, and expected length of the visit.
- **Ask about fees in advance if you want to.** It is reasonable to ask how the practice structures its membership and what is included. This article does not quote prices because they vary and change.
- **Bring a notebook or use your phone.** Write down answers so you can review them later.
- **Decide who will attend.** If a partner, family member, or caregiver helps with your care, consider bringing them.

## Questions Worth Asking

These questions are designed to help you understand a practice's structure and style. They are not a script, and you do not need to ask all of them.

1. How would you describe your approach to primary care?
2. What does a typical visit look like in terms of length and format?
3. How do you handle urgent concerns that come up between scheduled visits?
4. What is included in the membership, and what is handled separately?
5. How do you coordinate with specialists or hospitals if I need them?
6. How do you communicate — phone, portal, email, text — and what should I expect for response times?
7. What is your approach when a concern is outside your scope?
8. How do you handle records and privacy?
9. What happens if I need to pause or end the membership?
10. Is there anything you would like to know about me that we have not covered?

If a provider cannot answer a question, that is useful information too. A good sign is a provider who is willing to say "I do not know, and here is how I would find out."

## Hypothetical Examples (Clearly Labeled as Examples)

These examples are invented to illustrate how preparation can work. They are not real people, real practices, or real outcomes.

**Example 1: The busy parent.** A hypothetical parent with two young children wants a primary care relationship that can handle quick questions between visits. Before the consultation, they write down three questions: how same-day concerns are handled, whether children can be seen, and how the practice communicates with schools if needed. They bring a list of the children's medications and allergies. During the visit, they take notes and later compare the answers with another practice.

**Example 2: The person managing a chronic condition.** A hypothetical adult with an ongoing condition wants a provider who will coordinate with a specialist. Before the consultation, they write a one-page summary of their history and a list of the specialists they see. They ask how the DPC practice shares information with those specialists and what happens if the specialist recommends a change. They do not expect the DPC provider to replace the specialist; they want to understand the working relationship.

**Example 3: The person new to DPC.** A hypothetical adult who has only used insurance-based primary care is unsure what DPC includes. They prepare by writing down what they currently pay for and what they want to keep. They ask the provider to walk through the membership structure step by step and to explain what is not included. They leave with a written summary of the conversation.

In each example, the value comes from preparation, not from any promise about outcomes.

## During the Consultation: How to Get the Most From It

- **Be honest about your priorities.** If cost, access, or communication style matters most, say so.
- **Ask follow-up questions.** If an answer is vague, ask for an example.
- **Take notes.** You will not remember everything later.
- **Notice how you feel.** A first consultation is also a chance to assess fit. Feeling rushed, dismissed, or confused is worth noting.
- **Do not expect a diagnosis or treatment plan.** A first consultation is usually about fit and logistics, not clinical decisions.
- **Ask what the next step would be if you decided to join.**

## After the Consultation: Making a Decision

Give yourself time to review your notes. A simple comparison table can help. List the practices you spoke with and the answers to your core questions. Then ask yourself:

- Did the provider answer my questions clearly?
- Did the practice's structure match what I need?
- Did I feel comfortable asking difficult questions?
- Do I understand what is included and what is not?
- Do I know what the next step would be?

If you are unsure, it is reasonable to schedule a second conversation or to ask for written information. You are not obligated to decide immediately.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Assuming all DPC practices are the same.** They are not. Structure, communication, and scope can vary.
- **Skipping the logistics questions.** Understanding how a practice operates day to day matters as much as the philosophy.
- **Relying on memory.** Write things down.
- **Feeling pressured.** A good fit should feel like a good fit, not a hard sell.
- **Treating the first consultation as a full clinical visit.** It usually is not, and expecting otherwise can lead to disappointment.

## A Final Word

Preparing for a first Direct Primary Care consultation does not require special expertise. It requires a short list, a few good questions, and a willingness to pay attention to how the conversation feels. Take your time, compare carefully, and choose a provider whose approach matches your needs. If you have specific health, legal, or financial questions, consult a qualified professional.
